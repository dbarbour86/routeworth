How RouteWorth Calculates Route Profit

The basic formula is simple: route payout minus operating costs. The tricky part is remembering every cost that matters while you are comparing offers, parked between dispatches, or trying to make a fast decision.

Net profit Route payout - fuel cost - maintenance cost

From there, RouteWorth turns the result into two numbers drivers can act on: profit per mile and effective hourly rate.

The Numbers You Need Before Accepting a Route

  • Route payout: The total amount offered for the delivery route.
  • Total miles: Include pickup, drop-off, deadhead, and return miles if they apply.
  • Estimated hours: Count drive time, waiting time, handoff time, and likely delays.
  • Vehicle MPG: Use your real-world average, not the best number from a perfect highway day.
  • Gas price: Use the current local price you actually pay.
  • Maintenance per mile: RouteWorth defaults to $0.15 per mile to account for wear, tires, oil, brakes, and depreciation.

What Makes a Medical Courier Route Worth It?

RouteWorth rates a route as good when the effective hourly rate is at least $30 and profit per mile is at least $1.25. That gives drivers a fast way to separate healthy offers from routes that only look good because the payout is large.

Why Medical Couriers Need Profit Per Mile

Medical courier work can include long distance driving, strict pickup windows, waiting at facilities, return trips, and unpaid repositioning. Profit per mile helps show whether the route is paying enough for the distance, not just enough for the task.

A $180 payout can be strong on a short route and weak on a long one. The mileage changes the story.

Why Effective Hourly Rate Matters

Hourly rate shows what the route pays after time is counted. If a route ties up six hours, it needs to pay differently than a route that takes ninety minutes. For contract drivers, time spent waiting, loading, confirming delivery, and returning to a useful area still matters.

Common Route Profit Mistakes

  • Counting only loaded miles and ignoring deadhead miles.
  • Using gross payout as if it were take-home pay.
  • Ignoring maintenance because it does not come out of pocket that same day.
  • Accepting a high payout route without checking how many hours it will consume.
  • Comparing routes by payout only instead of profit per mile and hourly return.
