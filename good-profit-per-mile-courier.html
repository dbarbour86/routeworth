How to Calculate Profit Per Mile

Start with the route payout. Subtract estimated fuel cost and maintenance cost. Then divide the remaining net profit by total miles.

Profit per mile (Payout - fuel - maintenance) / total miles

Total miles should include more than the loaded miles shown in an offer. If you have to drive to pickup, return home, reposition after drop-off, or cover unpaid deadhead miles, those miles affect the real route value.

Example: Why Payout Can Be Misleading

A route paying $180 can sound strong. But if it requires 160 total miles, $28 in fuel, and $24 in maintenance, the route clears $128 before other taxes or business expenses.

Example $128 net / 160 miles = $0.80 per mile

That is not terrible, but it is borderline. A driver may still accept it if the hourly rate is good, the route stacks with another delivery, or it ends near another profitable area.

Gross Pay Per Mile vs Profit Per Mile

Gross pay per mile is payout divided by mileage. It is easy to calculate, but it can make a route look better than it really is. Profit per mile is more useful because it subtracts operating costs first.

  • Gross pay per mile: payout divided by miles.
  • Profit per mile: payout minus fuel and maintenance, divided by miles.

When a Lower Profit Per Mile Might Still Work

A borderline route is not always an automatic no. It can still make sense when it is fast, predictable, near your next pickup, part of a multi-route stack, or helping you avoid unpaid deadhead driving.

That is why RouteWorth checks both profit per mile and effective hourly rate. A smart route decision needs both numbers.

What Makes a Route Risky?

  • Long mileage with a payout that barely covers costs.
  • Unpaid waiting at labs, pharmacies, clinics, or facilities.
  • Return miles that are not counted in the offer.
  • High fuel prices or poor real-world MPG.
  • Routes that block you from better-paying work during peak hours.
